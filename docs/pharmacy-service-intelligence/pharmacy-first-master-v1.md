## 1. SERVICE OVERVIEW

### What Pharmacy First is

Pharmacy First is an NHS advanced service in England that allows patients to access assessment and treatment for seven common conditions directly from a participating community pharmacy, without needing a GP appointment first. Trained pharmacists use approved Patient Group Directions (PGDs) and clinical assessment tools to decide whether treatment can be supplied in the pharmacy, whether the patient should be referred to a GP, or whether urgent or emergency care is needed.

The service covers: acute sore throat (including suspected streptococcal infection), acute otitis media (earache) in children and young people, impetigo, infected insect bites and stings, shingles, acute sinusitis, and uncomplicated urinary tract infection (UTI) in women aged 16–64. Consultations take place in a private consultation room. Where treatment is appropriate, the pharmacist can supply prescription-only medicines (including antibiotics where indicated by pathway) at NHS expense, with no prescription charge for eligible NHS patients.

Pharmacy First sits alongside other NHS community pharmacy services (New Medicine Service, contraception, blood pressure checks, etc.) and is designed to reduce pressure on general practice and urgent care while keeping care close to where people live.

### Why it exists

Many presentations to GP surgeries and minor injury units are for self-limiting or straightforward infections that can be safely assessed and treated in primary care with the right clinical tools. Pharmacy First formalises pharmacist-led assessment so patients receive timely treatment, appropriate antibiotic stewardship is applied via pathway criteria, and general practice capacity is preserved for more complex care.

The service also addresses access: pharmacies often offer longer opening hours, walk-in availability, and locations in high streets and neighbourhoods where appointment waits for GP care can be lengthy.

### NHS objectives

- Reduce unnecessary GP appointments for seven defined acute conditions.
- Improve access to timely assessment and treatment in the community.
- Support antimicrobial stewardship through structured clinical pathways and documentation.
- Direct patients to the right level of care (self-care, pharmacy treatment, GP, urgent care).
- Increase utilisation of community pharmacy clinical capability within the NHS contractual framework.
- Improve patient experience through convenient, private consultations.
- Capture consultation data for service evaluation and pathway refinement.

### Patient benefits

- **Same-day access** — Many patients can be seen without booking a GP appointment days ahead.
- **No prescription charge** — For NHS-eligible patients, medicines supplied under Pharmacy First are free at the point of supply (standard NHS exemptions apply).
- **Private consultation** — Assessment occurs in a dedicated consultation room with a trained pharmacist.
- **Clear next steps** — Patients leave with a treatment plan, safety-netting advice, or a documented referral if needed.
- **Local convenience** — Community pharmacies are often closer to home or work than GP hubs.
- **Extended hours** — Many pharmacies open evenings and weekends when GP surgeries are closed.
- **Reduced A&E attendance** — Appropriate cases are managed in pharmacy rather than defaulting to urgent care.
- **Structured clinical assessment** — Decisions follow NHS-approved pathways, not ad hoc advice.
- **Antibiotic when clinically indicated** — Patients who meet criteria can receive antibiotics without a separate GP visit.
- **Safety-netting** — Pharmacists explain when to return, seek GP review, or attend urgent care.

### Pharmacy benefits

- **Expanded clinical role** — Pharmacists practise at the top of their capability using PGDs and assessment frameworks.
- **NHS remuneration** — The service is funded under the community pharmacy contractual framework.
- **Patient loyalty** — Successful consultations build trust and repeat engagement with the pharmacy.
- **Reduced friction** — Patients who might otherwise leave untreated or visit multiple providers can be resolved in one visit.
- **Professional satisfaction** — Direct patient impact through diagnosis-linked treatment decisions.
- **Integration with primary care** — Referral pathways strengthen relationships with local GP practices.
- **Data and audit trail** — Structured documentation supports governance and continuous improvement.
- **Workforce development** — Staff training in clinical pathways enhances overall pharmacy capability.

## 2. CONDITIONS COVERED

### 2.1 Acute Sore Throat (including suspected streptococcal infection)

**Description:**  
Acute sore throat is inflammation of the pharynx, usually viral. A subset is caused by Group A Streptococcus (GAS), which may benefit from antibiotic treatment to shorten symptoms and reduce complications in selected patients. Pharmacy First uses clinical scoring (e.g. FeverPAIN or Centor-style criteria within the NHS pathway) to identify patients likely to have bacterial infection who may be offered antibiotics.

**Typical symptoms:**  
Pain on swallowing, red or swollen throat, fever, absence of cough (more suggestive of GAS), swollen tender anterior cervical lymph nodes, tonsillar exudate or swelling, general malaise, headache.

**When Pharmacy First is appropriate:**  
Adults and children aged 5 years and over with acute sore throat symptoms starting within the last few days, where the patient can be assessed in pharmacy and meets pathway inclusion criteria. Pharmacist completes scoring, excludes red flags, and may supply analgesia and/or antibiotics per PGD if criteria are met.

**When GP referral is required:**  
Symptoms persisting beyond expected recovery despite appropriate treatment, recurrent severe sore throats suggesting need for further investigation, suspected glandular fever or other non-pathway diagnosis, immunocompromised patients outside PGD scope, children under 5, patients who fail to improve within safety-netting timeframe, suspected quinsy (peritonsillar abscess) or other complications.

**When urgent care is required:**  
Severe difficulty breathing or swallowing, drooling, stridor, rapidly worsening throat swelling, severe dehydration, signs of sepsis, airway compromise, unwell immunocompromised patient with systemic features.

### 2.2 Acute Otitis Media (Earache)

**Description:**  
Acute otitis media (AOM) is infection of the middle ear, common in children. It often follows upper respiratory infection. Many cases are viral and self-limiting; antibiotics are reserved for cases meeting NHS pathway criteria (e.g. bilateral AOM in children under 2, otorrhoea, systemic unwellness, or symptoms not improving).

**Typical symptoms:**  
Ear pain (young children may pull at the ear or be irritable), fever, hearing reduction, sometimes ear discharge if tympanic membrane has perforated, disturbed sleep, reduced feeding in infants (though infants under pathway age limits are excluded).

**When Pharmacy First is appropriate:**  
Children aged 1 to 17 years with symptoms suggestive of AOM within pathway timeframe, where otoscopic assessment is possible (or clinical judgement per local protocol), exclusion criteria are absent, and parent/carer can consent. Treatment may include analgesia and/or antibiotics per PGD.

**When GP referral is required:**  
Recurrent AOM requiring audiology or ENT review, suspected mastoiditis, hearing loss persisting after infection, grommets or complex ENT history outside PGD, children who do not meet age criteria, failed treatment with worsening symptoms, immunocompromised patients.

**When urgent care is required:**  
Swelling, redness or tenderness behind the ear (mastoid area), severe headache with neck stiffness, facial nerve weakness, meningism, sepsis, rapidly deteriorating child, severe dehydration.

### 2.3 Impetigo

**Description:**  
Impetigo is a superficial bacterial skin infection, usually caused by Staphylococcus aureus or Streptococcus pyogenes. It presents as golden crusted lesions, often around nose, mouth, or on exposed skin. It is contagious. Pharmacy First allows assessment and supply of topical (and in defined cases oral) antibiotic treatment.

**Typical symptoms:**  
Red sores that quickly rupture, honey-coloured or golden crusts, itchy or mildly painful lesions, spreading clusters, sometimes regional lymph node tenderness. Non-bullous impetigo is most common in pathway scope.

**When Pharmacy First is appropriate:**  
Patients with suspected non-bullous impetigo limited in extent per pathway (typically up to three lesions or localised area as defined in PGD), age 1 year and over, no signs of deeper skin infection, patient or carer able to apply topical treatment and follow hygiene advice.

**When GP referral is required:**  
Widespread impetigo beyond PGD limits, bullous impetigo, recurrent impetigo suggesting underlying condition (e.g. eczema flare, scabies), lesions near eyes, no improvement after completed treatment course, immunocompromised patients outside scope.

**When urgent care is required:**  
Rapidly spreading cellulitis, red streaking, severe pain, fever with systemically unwell patient, periorbital swelling affecting vision, signs of sepsis.

### 2.4 Infected Insect Bites and Stings

**Description:**  
Most insect bites cause local inflammatory reaction. Infection occurs when bacteria enter broken skin. Pharmacy First covers localised infected bites where spreading cellulitis is not present and systemic illness is absent.

**Typical symptoms:**  
Increasing pain, warmth, redness and swelling at bite site after initial improvement or after 48+ hours, pus or crusting, lymphangitis absent (if present, usually outside pathway), mild fever in some cases within pathway limits.

**When Pharmacy First is appropriate:**  
Age 1 year and over, clear history of insect bite or sting, localised infection signs within pathway definition, onset within specified days, no signs of systemic infection or widespread cellulitis. Topical or oral antibiotics per PGD where indicated.

**When GP referral is required:**  
Multiple infected sites, bite on face with significant swelling (especially periorbital), known allergy history requiring different management, no response to treatment, uncertain diagnosis (e.g. tick bite with erythema migrans suggesting Lyme disease).

**When urgent care is required:**  
Anaphylaxis or angioedema after sting, widespread urticaria with breathing difficulty, spreading cellulitis, red streaks (lymphangitis), severe systemic symptoms, suspected necrotising infection.

### 2.5 Shingles (Herpes Zoster)

**Description:**  
Shingles is reactivation of varicella-zoster virus in a dermatomal distribution. Early antiviral treatment within 72 hours of rash onset can reduce severity and complications in eligible patients. Pharmacy First enables prompt assessment and antiviral supply where criteria are met.

**Typical symptoms:**  
Unilateral dermatomal rash with vesicles on erythematous base, pain, burning or tingling (may precede rash), headache, malaise. Ophthalmic involvement is a referral concern.

**When Pharmacy First is appropriate:**  
Adults aged 18 and over with suspected shingles rash within 72 hours of onset (or up to 7 days if new vesicles still appearing, per pathway), rash consistent with dermatomal pattern, not immunocompromised outside PGD scope, no eye involvement.

**When GP referral is required:**  
Rash near eye (ophthalmic zoster), immunocompromised patient, severe or atypical presentation, pain not controlled, post-herpetic neuralgia requiring ongoing management, rash beyond 7 days without new vesicles where antivirals less beneficial.

**When urgent care is required:**  
Eye pain, visual changes, or vesicles on tip of nose (Hutchinson sign), meningitis symptoms, widespread rash in immunocompromised patient, severe headache with focal neurology.

---

### 2.6 Acute Sinusitis

**Description:**  
Acute sinusitis is inflammation of paranasal sinuses, usually following viral upper respiratory infection. Most cases resolve without antibiotics. Pharmacy First identifies patients with symptoms persisting beyond typical viral duration or with features suggesting bacterial sinusitis who may benefit from antibiotics per pathway.

**Typical symptoms:**  
Blocked or runny nose, facial pain or pressure (worse on bending forward), reduced smell, purulent nasal discharge, cough (often worse at night), headache, toothache (upper jaw), fever.

**When Pharmacy First is appropriate:**  
Adults and children aged 12 years and over, symptoms for minimum duration per pathway (typically 10+ days or worsening after initial improvement), bilateral facial pain with purulent discharge, or high fever with acute onset, exclusion of complications and red flags.

**When GP referral is required:**  
Recurrent sinusitis, suspected complications, symptoms not improving after antibiotic course, unilateral symptoms suggesting dental source, polyp or structural issues, immunocompromised patients.

**When urgent care is required:**  
Periorbital swelling, proptosis, visual changes, severe frontal headache with high fever (intracranial complication concern), altered mental state, meningism, severe unwellness.

---

### 2.7 Uncomplicated Urinary Tract Infection (Women 16–64)

**Description:**  
Uncomplicated lower UTI (cystitis) in non-pregnant women aged 16–64 is commonly caused by E. coli. Pharmacy First allows assessment via symptom checklist and dipstick urinalysis where available, with antibiotic supply when criteria are met.

**Typical symptoms:**  
Dysuria (pain on urination), frequency, urgency, suprapubic discomfort, cloudy or strong-smelling urine, sometimes haematuria. Absence of upper UTI features (flank pain, fever) and absence of complicating factors.

**When Pharmacy First is appropriate:**  
Woman aged 16–64, not pregnant, no catheter, no known structural urological abnormality, no recurrent UTIs beyond pathway threshold, typical lower UTI symptoms, positive nitrite/leucocyte on dipstick or strong clinical picture per pathway, able to take oral antibiotics.

**When GP referral is required:**  
Recurrent UTIs (typically three or more in 12 months per pathway), pregnancy, male patient (different pathway), blood in urine persisting, symptoms post-intercourse pattern needing investigation, failed antibiotic course, suspected STI, interstitial cystitis pattern.

**When urgent care is required:**  
Flank pain, fever and rigors, nausea and vomiting with systemic illness (pyelonephritis), confusion in elderly (not in this age band but safety-net broadly), sepsis, inability to retain fluids.

## 3. ELIGIBILITY

### NHS eligibility

- Patient must be registered with an NHS GP in England (or otherwise eligible for NHS primary care services).
- Consultation must be for one of the seven Pharmacy First conditions.
- Patient must present in person at a participating pharmacy (unless local policy allows video triage ahead of attendance — attendance still required for supply under PGD in most cases).
- Pharmacist must confirm identity and eligibility; carers may attend on behalf of children within age rules.

### Age restrictions (per condition)

| Condition | Age range (Pharmacy First pathway) |
|-----------|-------------------------------------|
| Sore throat | 5 years and over |
| Acute otitis media | 1–17 years |
| Impetigo | 1 year and over |
| Infected insect bites | 1 year and over |
| Shingles | 18 years and over |
| Acute sinusitis | 12 years and over |
| Uncomplicated UTI | Women 16–64 years |

### Exclusions

- Patients outside age bands for the specific condition.
- Pregnancy (for UTI and some other pathways — refer to GP/midwife).
- Immunocompromised patients (defined in PGD — often GP referral).
- Signs of serious complication or systemic sepsis.
- Conditions outside the seven pathways (e.g. chest infection, conjunctivitis unless separate service).
- Patients already on conflicting medication or with allergy to first-line pathway treatment without suitable alternative in PGD.
- Suspected non-pathway diagnosis (e.g. Lyme disease, quinsy, pyelonephritis).

### Special circumstances

- **Pregnancy:** UTI in pregnancy requires GP/midwife — not Pharmacy First. Other conditions in pregnancy generally referred to GP.
- **Immunocompromised:** HIV, chemotherapy, high-dose steroids, etc. — usually GP or specialist referral; shingles and infections may need urgent assessment.
- **Breastfeeding:** Some antibiotics compatible; pharmacist assesses per PGD and BNF; complex cases referred.
- **Allergy:** Penicillin allergy history assessed; alternative agents per pathway where available.
- **Repeat episodes:** Recurrent UTI, recurrent impetigo, recurrent sore throat — may exceed PGD and need GP investigation.
- **Children:** Parent/carer consent required; age limits strictly applied per condition.
- **Care homes:** Eligible if patient meets criteria; documentation and liaison with care staff may apply.

## 4. APPOINTMENT PROCESS

### Before appointment

- Patient identifies symptoms matching one of the seven conditions.
- Contact participating pharmacy by phone, walk-in, or online booking where available.
- Bring NHS number if known, list of current medicines, allergy information, and details of symptom onset.
- For UTI pathway: be ready to provide urine sample if pharmacy requests dipstick testing.
- For children: parent/carer must attend with child.

### During appointment

- Patient checked in at pharmacy counter; directed to private consultation room.
- Pharmacist confirms consent, identity, and presenting complaint.
- Confidential discussion of symptoms, medical history, red-flag screening, and medicines/allergies.
- Physical assessment as required (throat examination, otoscopy if available, skin inspection, shingles rash review, sinus symptom checklist, urinalysis for UTI).
- Outcome explained clearly: treat in pharmacy, self-care only, or refer.

### Assessment process

- Structured questions from NHS clinical pathway checklist.
- Scoring tools where applicable (e.g. sore throat FeverPAIN/Centor elements).
- Exclusion of referral and emergency criteria documented.
- Decision recorded in pharmacy clinical system for NHS reporting.
- Patient verbal consent for any treatment supplied under PGD.

### Treatment options

- **Self-care and advice:** Analgesia (paracetamol/ibuprofen), fluids, rest, hygiene measures (impetigo), cold compress (insect bites).
- **Pharmacy-supplied medicines under PGD:** Antibiotics (phenoxymethylpenicillin, doxycycline, flucloxacillin, clarithromycin, nitrofurantoin, etc. per condition), topical antibiotics (fusidic acid), antivirals (aciclovir for shingles), as pathway indicates.
- **No supply:** Referral to GP with referral note or urgent care directive if red flags present.

### Follow up process

- Safety-netting advice: expected improvement timeline, when to return to pharmacy, when to see GP, when to call 111 or attend A&E.
- Completed course of antibiotics if supplied — patient advised not to stop early unless adverse reaction.
- Contact details for pharmacy if symptoms worsen.
- GP notification may be sent electronically or via referral form depending on local integration.
- If no improvement within 48–72 hours (condition-specific), patient advised to seek GP review.

## 6. COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS

**Myth:** Pharmacy First is the same as asking the pharmacist for advice over the counter.  
**Reality:** It is a formal NHS clinical service with structured assessment, documentation, and PGD-supply of prescription-only medicines where criteria are met.

**Myth:** Any pharmacy in England must offer Pharmacy First.  
**Reality:** Only pharmacies that have signed up and trained staff to deliver the advanced service can provide it.

**Myth:** Pharmacists give antibiotics to everyone with a sore throat.  
**Reality:** Antibiotics are supplied only when scoring and pathway criteria suggest bacterial infection likely; many sore throats are viral.

**Myth:** Pharmacy First replaces your GP entirely.  
**Reality:** It covers seven specific conditions; GPs remain essential for ongoing care, chronic disease, and complex presentations.

**Myth:** You always need a GP prescription first.  
**Reality:** For eligible conditions, the pharmacist can supply antibiotics directly under PGD without a GP prescription.

**Myth:** Men can get UTI treatment through Pharmacy First.  
**Reality:** The UTI pathway is for women aged 16–64 only; men with UTI symptoms need GP assessment.

**Myth:** Children of any age can use all pathways.  
**Reality:** Each condition has minimum and maximum ages; e.g. sore throat from age 5, UTI women 16–64 only.

**Myth:** Shingles treatment works the same if you start antivirals two weeks later.  
**Reality:** Antivirals are most effective within 72 hours of rash onset; delay reduces benefit.

**Myth:** Infected insect bite means you always need oral antibiotics.  
**Reality:** Many local reactions are inflammatory not infected; pathway distinguishes localised infection needing treatment.

**Myth:** Sinusitis always needs antibiotics.  
**Reality:** Most acute sinusitis is viral; antibiotics only when duration and features meet bacterial criteria.

**Myth:** Pharmacy First consultations are not confidential.  
**Reality:** They take place in private consultation rooms with the same confidentiality duties as other healthcare.

**Myth:** You pay a consultation fee like a private clinic.  
**Reality:** For NHS-eligible patients, the service and supplied medicines under PGD are free at point of care.

**Myth:** Pharmacists cannot examine your throat or ears.  
**Reality:** Trained pharmacists perform clinical examination appropriate to the pathway, including otoscopy where equipment is available.

**Myth:** If dipstick is negative, you cannot have a UTI.  
**Reality:** Pathway allows clinical judgement in defined circumstances; negative dipstick may still lead to GP referral.

**Myth:** Impetigo is just dry skin and does not need treatment.  
**Reality:** Impetigo is bacterial and contagious; treatment reduces spread and complications.

**Myth:** You can get Pharmacy First medicines delivered like online pharmacies.  
**Reality:** Assessment is in person at a participating community pharmacy in most cases.

**Myth:** Antibiotics from pharmacy are weaker than GP prescriptions.  
**Reality:** The same first-line NHS antibiotics are used per national pathway.

**Myth:** Pharmacy First is only for people who cannot get a GP appointment.  
**Reality:** It is for anyone meeting criteria who chooses convenient community access.

**Myth:** Recurrent UTIs are always treated at pharmacy.  
**Reality:** Recurrence beyond pathway threshold requires GP investigation.

**Myth:** Earache in a baby under 1 year can be treated at pharmacy.  
**Reality:** AOM pathway starts at age 1; younger infants need GP or urgent assessment.

**Myth:** Shingles near the eye can be managed in pharmacy.  
**Reality:** Ophthalmic involvement requires urgent GP or eye service referral.

**Myth:** Pharmacists do not tell your GP what happened.  
**Reality:** Referrals and notifications are part of integrated care; GP may receive record of consultation.

**Myth:** You need to be very ill to use Pharmacy First.  
**Reality:** The service is for uncomplicated presentations; very unwell patients need urgent or emergency care.

**Myth:** Two pharmacies can treat the same episode twice with antibiotics.  
**Reality:** Pathway and safety-netting aim to prevent inappropriate repeat supply; history is checked.

**Myth:** Pharmacy First covers conjunctivitis.  
**Reality:** Conjunctivitis is not one of the seven conditions unless locally commissioned separately.

**Myth:** Cold sores and shingles are treated the same way.  
**Reality:** Different conditions; shingles pathway is for herpes zoster dermatomal rash in eligible adults.

**Myth:** Ibuprofen alone cures ear infections.  
**Reality:** Analgesia helps pain; antibiotics only when pathway criteria for bacterial AOM are met.

**Myth:** Golden crust on skin is always eczema.  
**Reality:** Honey-coloured crusts suggest impetigo, which needs antibacterial treatment.

**Myth:** Pharmacy First is a private paid service from Boots etc.  
**Reality:** It is an NHS England advanced service available at participating contractors including independents and multiples.

**Myth:** You must know which condition you have before visiting.  
**Reality:** Patient describes symptoms; pharmacist assesses whether a pathway applies.

**Myth:** Antivirals for shingles cure the pain immediately.  
**Reality:** Antivirals reduce viral replication; pain may persist and need separate analgesia.

**Myth:** Pregnant women can use UTI pathway.  
**Reality:** Pregnancy excludes UTI pathway; GP or midwife must assess.

**Myth:** Immunosuppressed patients are automatically treated in pharmacy.  
**Reality:** Many immunocompromised patients are excluded and referred for higher-level care.

**Myth:** Pharmacy First staff are not clinically trained.  
**Reality:** Pharmacists are qualified independent prescribers-level clinicians for PGD; technicians support under supervision.

**Myth:** A red insect bite always means infection.  
**Reality:** Early bites are often inflamed; infection signs develop later with increasing pain and spreading warmth.

**Myth:** Sinusitis means you need a scan at pharmacy.  
**Reality:** Diagnosis is clinical; imaging is not part of pharmacy pathway.

**Myth:** You cannot use Pharmacy First if you already saw GP.  
**Reality:** If still within pathway and not already treated appropriately, pharmacy may assess — unless exclusion applies.

**Myth:** Pharmacists rush consultations to sell products.  
**Reality:** NHS service has defined clinical standards and remuneration per consultation, not product upselling.

**Myth:** All earache is otitis media.  
**Reality:** Other causes exist; pharmacist assesses and refers if diagnosis unclear.

**Myth:** Streptococcal throat always has white spots.  
**Reality:** Scoring uses multiple features; appearance alone does not determine antibiotics.

**Myth:** Pharmacy First is available 24/7 everywhere.  
**Reality:** Hours depend on individual pharmacy opening times.

**Myth:** Online symptom checkers replace pharmacy visit.  
**Reality:** Supply under PGD requires pharmacist assessment in participating pharmacy.

**Myth:** Children need blood tests for UTI at pharmacy.  
**Reality:** UTI pathway in women 16–64 uses symptoms and dipstick, not blood tests.

**Myth:** Shingles vaccine means you cannot get Pharmacy First for shingles.  
**Reality:** Vaccination reduces risk but breakthrough cases may still occur and be assessed if eligible.

**Myth:** Referral from pharmacy means you failed.  
**Reality:** Referral is appropriate care when presentation is outside PGD or needs investigation.

**Myth:** One Pharmacy First visit treats the whole family for impetigo.  
**Reality:** Each patient needs individual assessment; household advice may be given for hygiene.

**Myth:** Antibiotics for insect bites are always seven days.  
**Reality:** Duration follows PGD and BNF for the agent supplied; pharmacist explains course length.

**Myth:** Pharmacy First data is sold to marketing companies.  
**Reality:** Clinical records are governed by NHS confidentiality and pharmacy professional standards.

**Myth:** You need medical insurance for Pharmacy First.  
**Reality:** It is an NHS service for eligible patients.

**Myth:** Pharmacists cannot refuse to give antibiotics if you demand them.  
**Reality:** Supply must meet clinical criteria; inappropriate demand is declined with explanation or referral.

## 9. TRUST CONTENT

### GPhC trust content

Every pharmacist supplying medicines under Pharmacy First is registered with the General Pharmaceutical Council (GPhC), the independent regulator for pharmacy professionals in Great Britain. Registration requires a Master of Pharmacy degree, pre-registration training, and passing a registration assessment. Registrants must meet continuing professional development requirements and adhere to Standards for Pharmacy Professionals covering person-centred care, privacy, honesty, and teamwork.

The GPhC inspects registered pharmacy premises against standards including governance, staff training, and suitability of consultation facilities. Patients can verify pharmacist registration on the GPhC online register. Fitness to practise proceedings hold pharmacists accountable for serious misconduct or incompetence. Professional indemnity insurance is mandatory for practising pharmacists.

Pharmacy First delivery requires additional NHS-mandated training beyond baseline registration, ensuring competence in clinical assessment, PGD use, and referral decision-making. The GPhC expects pharmacists to recognise limits of competence and refer when presentations fall outside their scope — this is a professional duty, not optional.

### NHS trust content

Pharmacy First is commissioned by NHS England as part of the community pharmacy contractual framework. It is not a commercial add-on but a defined advanced service with national service specifications, clinical pathways, and payment mechanisms. Participating pharmacies contractually agree to deliver the service to NHS standards.

Clinical pathways align with NICE guidance where available and are approved by NHS England medical directors for PGD use. Medicines supplied are NHS standard formulary choices — the same agents GPs would typically prescribe first line. Consultation outcomes contribute to NHS monitoring and quality assurance.

Patients retain full NHS rights: free care at point of delivery for eligible individuals, confidentiality under common law and GDPR, and right to complain via NHS complaints procedure and Parliamentary and Health Service Ombudsman route if unresolved.

### Pharmacist expertise content

Pharmacists are medicines experts with university training in pharmacology, therapeutics, clinical biochemistry, and patient communication. Many completing Pharmacy First have additional credentials in independent prescribing or clinical pharmacy diplomas. They interpret symptoms in context of drug interactions, renal function, pregnancy, and allergy — a depth retail advice cannot match.

In consultation, pharmacists do not simply "sell antibiotics." They apply differential diagnosis reasoning: is this viral or bacterial? Is this impetigo or eczema flare? Is this lower UTI or pyelonephritis? This clinical reasoning is documented and auditable. Pharmacists also advise on non-drug measures: analgesia dosing, impetigo hygiene, shingles pain management, and sinus steam inhalation where appropriate.

### Consultation room content

The consultation room is a dedicated private space meeting NHS requirements for confidentiality and dignity. Conversations cannot be overheard on the shop floor. Visual assessment of rash, throat, or skin occurs with appropriate chaperoning offered for vulnerable patients. Equipment may include otoscope, torch, tongue depressors, urinalysis dipsticks, and clinical thermometers.

Patients should expect the pharmacist to take a full history before examination, explain findings in plain language, and summarise the plan before leaving. Duration is sufficient for thorough assessment — typically not a brief counter conversation. Records are kept securely in pharmacy clinical systems.

### Community pharmacy trust content

Community pharmacies have served neighbourhoods for decades, often longer than many GP practices in the same location. They are accessible without appointment and see the community across health and illness. Pharmacy First formalises clinical care many patients already trusted pharmacists for informally.

Independent and small chain pharmacies invest in local relationships — same staff year after year, knowledge of local GPs, and participation in health campaigns. NHS payment for Pharmacy First supports sustainable clinical services rather than relying on retail margin alone. Patients choosing community pharmacy support local employment and keep healthcare assets on the high street.
